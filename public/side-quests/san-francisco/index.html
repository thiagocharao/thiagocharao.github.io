I Left My Feet in San Francisco

One week, one 10 kg carry-on, three burgers, a foul ball, a present for Clarissa from the Warriors shop, and a bridge I had to go back and say goodbye to.

Porto → Lisbon → SFOGitHub Universe '26Hotel on Lombard StWarriors vs Lakers

San Francisco had been on my list since I was a kid, long before I knew what a Clipper card was. It got there the way most 90s dreams did, through a television in Brazil: Dinosaurs, Full House, and every California film that came after, plus a side dream of studying at Stanford that never happened and never quite went away. The trip finally happened the way these things do: an invitation to a conference that turned up in my inbox, a visa that lived in the wrong passport, a €642 fare that left at five in the morning, and a wife who drafted the first itinerary before I had finished saying yes.

This is the week as it actually went, pieced together from the messages I sent to Claude, who rode along in my pocket, from the gym, the bridge, the ferry, a cable car stop I could not work out, a steakhouse that would not let me in, a basketball arena that would not let my bag in, and a steakhouse that would. Planned things get crossed out. Real things get photos. My own typos stay in, because they are evidence.

Photos by my phone. Spelling by my thumbs. Timeline by my credit card, which remembered things I did not. Patience by Claude.

Prologue0

Two passports, one visa, and the wrong Mac

In which I book the trip in a weekend, Claude drives the wrong computer, and nobody can buy the workshop with one seat left.

It started with an email. The conference pass was waived thanks to Max, which I reported with the enthusiasm of a man who has just saved $1,399: "got the invite finally, so I don't pay for it." The visa was fine. The visa was also in my old passport, which meant travelling with both passports in the same pocket for the rest of the week, a line that would appear in every checklist from then on.

The flight was a €642 TAP fare leaving Porto at 05:00, which Claude described as brutal for most people but fine for someone who lives ten minutes from the airport. The fare was the Discount kind: carry-on only, ten kilos. Remember the ten kilos.

Clarissa drafted the first itinerary in her own chat. Alcatraz turned out to be closed for the exact week. The Warriors were playing the Lakers on the Friday. Section 222, row 8, seat 11.

Then came the Saturday before departure, when I asked Claude to log in to the conference portal and book my sessions. The browser pane stayed blank. I switched settings. I switched browsers. I typed a sentence that should be framed. Claude, it turned out, had been faithfully opening browsers on the Mac Studio in the other room while I sat at the MacBook. Once the right computer was found, 18 sessions were booked in ten minutes.

The one thing that could never be booked was the AI credits workshop, which showed "1 of 30 seats left" every single time Claude checked, five times across the week, like a fruit machine that only ever shows two cherries.

Twenty-three hours door to door, then a palace

Alarm at 02:40. Clocks changed overnight. Lisbon's arrivals hall visited by mistake. A quiet room. A first burger.

I went to bed at seven the night before and left the house at ten past three, with a plan to train every morning in San Francisco. Hold that thought. In Lisbon I landed, walked confidently out through arrivals, and sent a message in Portuguese asking whether I should now go to Terminal 2. I should not. I went back in.

Somewhere over the Atlantic, Claude rebuilt my Google Maps lists from the 402 places I have saved around Portugal, which is how it learned that I like smash burgers, picanha, francesinha, specialty coffee and cocktail bars you need to be told about. It also tried to book House of Prime Rib. House of Prime Rib does not take online bookings for one. Note this. It will come back.

My first message from American soil was not "I landed." It was "best burgers in sf!" The hotel had read the email about the noisy road and given me a quiet room. They asked if I wanted full housekeeping or just towels. I went with full service, like a man on holiday.

I treated the jet lag the correct way: a walk in the last of the light to the Palace of Fine Arts, where the rotunda was lit and doubled in the lagoon, then a burger at Super Duper on Chestnut, then, according to my card, two rounds at Sully's on Chestnut at $15.38 each, which is where the Negroni count quietly started. Bed at 21:46. My wrist tracker gave the night an 87 percent and said nothing about the burger. It would have plenty to say later.

The day my feet filed a complaint

Gym, crooked street, roast beef, bus, bridge, headland, ferry, burger, Waymo, tower, cable car, badge, prime rib, Negroni. In that order. In one day.

The day began at a gym inside a former Moorish cinema on Polk Street, because of course it did. Day pass, $35, session ran long. This was the first of the daily training sessions I had promised myself. It was also the last. The wrist tracker logged the day at a strain of 18.0 and 29,918 steps, which is what happens when you follow a gym session with a bridge, and my body quietly cancelled the rest of the programme on my behalf. From there I walked up to Lombard, looked down the crooked block, walked down the crooked block, and stopped at Lucca on Chestnut for a roast beef sandwich I described as breakfast, then at Twisted St. Cafe for the coffee I had been hunting since 06:22.

Then the bridge. I asked whether the bus went "through the water" like I would have walked, which is a fair question about a bus. It went through the Presidio instead. The day was clear, no fog, the Marin hills sharp behind the towers, and at 11:31 I sent two words: "starting to cross now."

At the far end the plan had me walking up to Battery Spencer for the photo and then down into Sausalito for the 13:35 ferry. The plan had not met my feet. A Lyft was summoned to a road my phone insisted on calling Con Woman Rd, stopped at the battery long enough for the full-length photo with the bridge behind me, and dropped me at the ferry with time to buy a drink. At 12:52 I filed the complaint: "my feet are killing me now 😅". Claude pointed out that it was the gym plus ten kilometres. This did not help.

On the ferry I made two decisions. I would skip the customer reception that evening, the invitation-only event my pass had been built around, because I wanted more San Francisco. And I would have a proper lunch. The proper lunch was a burger at Gott's at the Ferry Building. Then, with no warning, "ha got waymo for the first time." I filmed it pulling up to the kerb like a nervous dad, then filmed the steering wheel turning on its own, and a car with nobody in the front took me to Coit Tower, where the card shows three separate visits to the gift shop in 22 minutes, which is what indecision costs: $4.60, $11 and $7.99.

Washington Square, then Caffe Trieste, the old North Beach espresso bar Claude had recommended, which is classic in every way including the one where it only takes cash. The ATM was at least inside. $23.50 withdrawn for a coffee and a pastry. Then Powell Street, where the cable car defeated me. "can't figure how to hop in the tram thingy." You wave at it, it turns out. I waved. Nine dollars, down Hyde Street with Alcatraz in the windscreen, to the end of the line at the water.

Badge collected, one drink at a crafthouse by the water, Negroni count rising. Then the quest. House of Prime Rib refused me two nights running. Monday I walked in and was told an hour and a half. Tuesday I walked in earlier, asked for a bar stool, and learned that a bar stool does not exist for a party of one on a Tuesday. Same hour and a half. "no go." Four minutes from the hotel, with no sign outside, The Brazen Head had a ten ounce prime rib on the menu, limited availability. I got it, with a loaded potato and a green salad with creamy Italian. Then: "got a negroni." The quest was over. It had taken one day.

Breakfast was Home Plate, one block from the hotel. Claude pushed the crab omelette with some force. What I ordered is lost to history, which tells you everything.

At Fort Mason the flags were up, the arch was glowing, and the catering had built towers of doughnuts under a screen that said "hitting new bottlenecks". The daily training plan was already a memory. I had a seat in a hands-on sandbox session that required queueing 30 minutes early. I skipped it for two sessions on agentic engineering, standby only, no seat guaranteed, and got in. At 11:00: "i'm on agentic one. amazing so far what's next?" The rest of the day was main stage, model routers, agents with credentials, and a slide from AMD and ING asking whether any of this is actually paying off, which is the question I get asked at work every week.

The useful discovery of the day, repeated over the next two: everyone in the building was fighting the same fights I fight. The pressure comes from the top for everyone. Nothing we are doing at Planet sounded strange to anyone. I would put this on LinkedIn the next day, after a joke about my suitcase. The card also shows a Logitech purchase at 12:48, which nobody remembers deciding, and a stop at Ghirardelli Square on the way to dinner, which everybody remembers.

Dinner was The Bird, a fried chicken sandwich counter in SoMa with the World Series on the screen. "made a friend at the event." The friend has a name, Rahul, and by Friday night he was texting from the game: "so competitive." It was a good night.

Caltrain 118, track 9, tap on with Clipper. "which stop do I get off? next is south SF." Palo Alto, after Menlo Park. I walked Palm Drive up to the Stanford quad, the campus a kid in Brazil had decided to study at and never did, stood in it for an hour, found the HP Garage, which is a garage behind a house on a quiet street where people live, took my photos from the far pavement at the pace of someone expecting a patrol car, and sat down at Local Union 271 on University Avenue for a half-pound duck fat burger, my third burger of the week, at which point I looked at photos of the Computer History Museum I had planned for three weeks and sent the most honest message of the trip: "i'll be honest… looked the pics of the museum… not really my thing." Back to the city, then.

I walked fifteen minutes up Fourth Street to the ballpark and found one there. It was an Uber. Lyft had cancelled on me once that day already and I hold grudges.

Saturday 31 October6

Ten kilos, a goodbye, a ribeye, and a nun

At 06:53 the ten kilos came due. Swag, a baseball in a cube, a satin jacket, a cap, Clarissa's Warriors things, Stanford things. "I hope I can manage thatZ." I managed it, the Z included.

The plan for the morning was the Ferry Plaza Farmers Market. I had already been. The alternative was the ocean end of the city, Lands End and the Sutro Baths. I had a coffee at Twisted instead, took the bus to Crissy Field with Clipper, which I now understood, bought $7 of postcards at the Warming Hut, and did the thing the whole week had been pointing at: I went back to the bridge. "came back to the bridge to say bye 🤗🤗." The photo, from the bluff above Fort Point with the clouds breaking over Marin, is the best one of the trip, and I took it with my phone, having left the real camera at home to save weight.

Lunch was always going to be a steak. A steakhouse on the Embarcadero was booked, then cancelled for something more San Francisco: Original Joe's in North Beach, red booths, open grill, Italian-American since 1937. Claude suggested the ribeye, medium-rare, green peppercorn sauce, house ravioli on the side. My report back: "got with fries and a salad with ranch on the side." Which is the correct American order, and the week in one sentence. The photos later revealed what the message left out: another Negroni next to the plate, and a bowl of warm sugared doughnuts with chocolate and raspberry to finish. I had packed to ten kilos that morning. The doughnuts did not count.

At the airport my new Revolut lounge pass, the one that made me "fancy now 😂😂", was turned away by two lounges in a row, one of them "for flight attendants only", before China Airlines took pity on me. The lounge was fine. The people in it were pretentious. I had the food and left. And then, at the gate, Halloween. The gate staff boarded us dressed as the nun from the horror films: one waving at the window and posing for everyone, another standing in the jet bridge in full habit, with glasses and a beard, checking boarding passes with great seriousness. The last photo I took in America is a demon nun waving goodbye. San Francisco knows how to end a week.

The Lyft to the airport cost, to the cent, what the Lyft from the airport had cost six days earlier. The city balanced its books. Then twelve hours to Lisbon, both passports in the same pocket, and a second lounge in Lisbon on Sunday morning, where the people were no less pretentious and the food was no less free. The clocks in America went back that night. Mine had already gone back a week earlier. For six days I had been seven hours away from home instead of eight, and I had used every one of them. The step counter says so: 102,837 steps, roughly 75 kilometres on foot, on legs that filed a formal complaint on day two.

Epilogue

I came for a conference and went back for a bridge

The conference was good. I learned that everyone has the same problems, which is the most reassuring thing a conference can teach you, and I said so in public. But the week was the other thing: the city a kid in Brazil had decided, somewhere between Dinosaurs and Full House, to see one day, seen on foot, by ferry, by robot taxi and by a cable car I had to be told to wave at.

I skipped the reception for more of it. I skipped the museum for more of it. On the last morning, with a packed bag and a steak reservation, I went back up the hill above Fort Point to look at the bridge one more time and say bye, with two hugging emoji, like you would to a person.

Next time: the camera comes, the feet get a day off, and Stanford gets an application.

Epílogo

Vim por uma conferência e voltei por uma ponte

A conferência foi boa. Aprendi que todo mundo tem os mesmos problemas, que é a coisa mais tranquilizadora que uma conferência pode ensinar, e disse isso em público. Mas a semana foi a outra coisa: a cidade que um menino no Brasil tinha decidido, em algum ponto entre Família Dinossauro e Três É Demais, conhecer um dia, conhecida a pé, de balsa, de táxi robô e de um bonde pro qual tive que ser ensinado a acenar.

Pulei a recepção por mais um pouco dela. Pulei o museu por mais um pouco dela. Na última manhã, com a mala feita e um bife reservado, subi de volta o morro acima do Fort Point pra olhar a ponte mais uma vez e dizer tchau, com dois emojis de abraço, como se faz com uma pessoa.

Da próxima vez: a câmera vem, os pés ganham um dia de folga, e Stanford recebe uma inscrição.
